Layer 1 · Forecasting

Forecasting for the Water Value

What the dispatch and bidding layers need from a price forecast, what we run today (Chronos-2 and a Gaussian copula), where it falls short, and the options for improving it: adaptive calibration, models shaped for the SDP, diffusion models, mixtures of experts.

§1 What the optimiser needs from a forecast

The SDP doesn't need a best guess. It needs the conditional law of future prices given what is known at decision time, \(\mathbb P(\lambda_{t+1:T} \mid \mathcal F_t)\), in a form it can use. The dispatch methods page derives why. Each requirement has a reason:

RequirementWhy the decision layer needs itWhere it is used
Marginal distribution per intervalOpen-loop value depends only on the mean (Fact 1), but the offer curve reacts to every possible priceState prices of the lattice, offer price grid
The mean, not just the medianRevenue is linear in price; skewed prices make the median understate value (reserves: realised mean ~14% above the forecast median)Point forecasts, reserve state prices
Dependence between stepsThe value of waiting comes from how much \(\lambda_t\) says about \(\lambda_{t+1}\). Independent steps create fake spreads (25 M expected vs 16 M perfect foresight)Markov transitions (\(\rho\))
Tails (spikes)A few spike intervals carry a large share of the profit; the top state sets how much energy to holdTop lattice states / nodes
Joint law of the four pricesReserves and energy compete for the same MW and SoCReserve factor states
Fresh every 30 minutesRe-forecasting is where real information enters; the chain inside one issue only models the next hoursRolling issues (17,567 per year)

The deciding metric is downstream profit, measured by running forecast → SDP → offers → simulator. CRPS and coverage are diagnostics. A forecast that improves CRPS but not profit isn't kept.

§3 From quantiles to paths: the Gaussian copula

Chronos-2 predicts quantiles directly; it doesn't sample paths. The copula supplies the dependence between steps. For each past issue and step \(h\), the realised price's position in its own forecast gives a uniform score and a normal score:

\[ u_{h} = \hat F_{h}(y_{h}) \in (0,1), \qquad z_h = \Phi^{-1}(u_h), \qquad \boldsymbol z = (z_1,\dots,z_{48}) \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol 0, \Sigma). \]

\(\Sigma\) is the correlation of these scores over the 90 days before the month (walk-forward), shrunk towards the identity: \(\Sigma = (1-\delta)\hat\Sigma + \delta I\), \(\delta = 0.02\) (the value whose scenarios match the realised lag-1 correlation best). A path is drawn by sampling \(\boldsymbol z\) and mapping each step back through its own quantile function:

\[ \lambda_h = \hat F_h^{-1}\big(\Phi(z_h)\big), \qquad h = 1,\dots,48. \]

The marginals stay exactly the forecast's; only the ranks are coupled. Between and beyond the 21 quantiles, \(\hat F^{-1}\) is interpolated and extrapolated (tails), which matters for the top states (§5).

§4 What the SDP actually consumes

The water-value SDP doesn't use the 30 scenarios. It uses the quantiles and one number of the copula:

Forecast outputSDP objectHow
21 quantiles per stepState prices \(\lambda_t(k)\)11 equal-probability bins (bin means), or 21 score nodes (local means, continuous state)
Copula lag-1 \(\rho\) (monthly)Transitions \(P(k' \mid k)\)AR(1) on normal scores: Tauchen bins or exact hat expectations
Reserve quantiles + error correlationsReserve states \(\pi^p_t(r)\)One common factor, loadings 0.63/0.45/0.78, 5 bins
ScenariosPrice band, expected SoC at gate closureTrim offers to the scenario band ± margin

So the dependence the SDP sees is a first-order Markov chain on normal scores. That's consistent with the copula only if \(\Sigma_{ij} \approx \rho^{|i-j|}\). Whether the forecast errors keep more memory than that (their partial autocorrelation at lags 2–3) is not yet measured.

§5 Known weaknesses

  • Bands slightly too narrow: 77% coverage for an 80% band.
  • Tails beyond the 99% quantile are extrapolated. The 11-bin top state averages the top 9%, so it's robust. The continuous state's top node sits at score 3 (top 0.25%) and inherits the extrapolation: 3,493 SGD on 15 Sep 2026 against 1,185 for the top bin.
  • Reserve medians undervalue reserves by ~14% (right skew). The mean fixes the point value.
  • Gaussian dependence: no tail dependence (spikes cluster more than a Gaussian copula allows), and one \(\Sigma\) per month regardless of regime.
  • Covariates: demand is past-only, and EMC pre-dispatch isn't a covariate yet. On a first, tiny sample (64 points, one night), Chronos-2 had half the error of pre-dispatch (MAE 22.7 vs 49.2) but pre-dispatch was better one half-hour ahead and biased in a learnable way.
  • No model of how the forecast itself evolves. The SDP's chain models realised prices, not forecast updates.

F1Calibration and adaptivity

FixesCoverage, bias, drift
EffortSmall
SDP fitDirect: better marginals and means

Adaptive conformal inference (ACI) keeps a band's coverage on target under drift by nudging the quantile level after each observation. For a target miss rate \(\alpha\) and step \(\gamma\):

\[ \alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma\,\big(\alpha - \mathbf 1[y_t \notin \text{band}_t(\alpha_t)]\big). \]

Applied per quantile level (or to the whole PIT distribution through a rolling isotonic map \(u \mapsto \tilde u\)), it turns 77% into 80% without retraining. Other cheap steps:

  • Online bias correction of the location by lead time and hour of day.
  • Online forecast combination on the CRPS (Bernstein online aggregation, “CRPS learning”): weights per quantile level move towards whichever source has been right lately.
  • Fine-tuning Chronos-2 on NEMS history (LoRA), within one regime, kept only if profit improves.

F2Tails and spikes

FixesTop states, spike timing
EffortSmall to medium
SDP fitDirect: the top states hold the value
  • Extreme-value tail. Beyond the 95% quantile, fit a generalised Pareto tail on past exceedances instead of extrapolating: \(\mathbb P(Y > q_{0.95} + y \mid Y > q_{0.95}) = (1 + \xi y/\sigma)^{-1/\xi}\), capped at the market price cap (4,500 SGD/MWh).
  • Spike probability model. A classifier for \(\mathbb P(\text{spike at } t \mid \mathcal F_s)\), mixed with the body: \(F = (1-\pi_t) F_{\text{body}} + \pi_t F_{\text{spike}}\). Spikes depend on reserve margins, outages and interties, which a classifier can use better than a quantile head.
  • Quantile gradient boosting on residuals, as the design doc planned, for the extreme deciles where foundation models are weakest.

F3Models shaped for the SDP

FixesDependence in the form the SDP uses
EffortMedium
SDP fitNative: they define the state and transitions

Instead of fitting a 48×48 copula and then reading one number from it, fit the dependence directly in the form the SDP consumes: a small Markov state.

  • Gaussian Markov copula. AR(1) or AR(2) on normal scores, with \(\rho\) by lead time or hour of day. Scenarios and the lattice then come from one model. First check the partial autocorrelation of the errors.
  • Regime switching. A hidden regime \(R_t \in \{\text{calm}, \text{tight}\}\) with transition matrix \(Q\), and a price model per regime (Hamilton; Janczura & Weron for electricity):
    \[ z_t \mid R_t = j \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\mu_j + \rho_j (z_{t-1} - \mu_j),\ \sigma_j^2\big), \qquad \mathbb P(R_{t+1} = j' \mid R_t = j) = Q_{jj'}. \]
    The SDP state becomes (regime, price bin). The regime is not observed, so in practice the state is the filtered probability \(\mathbb P(R_t = \text{tight} \mid \mathcal F_t)\), binned. This captures spike clustering, which a single AR(1) cannot.
  • Slow factor plus fast noise. \(z_t = a L_t + \sqrt{1-a^2}\, u_t\), with \(L\) a slowly moving level (a forecast that is off all day) and \(u\) fast AR(1) noise. Two coordinates of the continuous state (M9).
  • Martingale model of forecast evolution. Model how the forecast for a fixed delivery interval changes from issue to issue: \(\hat\lambda_{t\mid s+1} = \hat\lambda_{t\mid s} + \varepsilon_{s+1,t}\), with the updates uncorrelated over \(s\). That's the right object for rolling decisions: it says how much a later decision will know, which is what the value of waiting depends on.
  • Transitions learnt from trajectories. Once paths are good, estimate the transition table directly (kernel regression on paths, page M9). No parametric form.

F4Generative paths: diffusion and flows

FixesNon-Gaussian, multivariate dependence
EffortLarge (research)
SDP fitThrough learnt transitions or LSMC

A diffusion model learns to turn noise into realistic paths by reversing a noising process. For a path \(\boldsymbol x\) conditioned on context \(\boldsymbol c\) (history, covariates), training minimises the denoising error

\[ \mathcal L = \mathbb E_{k,\boldsymbol x,\boldsymbol\epsilon}\Big[\big\|\boldsymbol\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\,\boldsymbol x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_k}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\ k,\ \boldsymbol c\big)\big\|^2\Big], \]

and sampling runs the learnt denoiser backwards from pure noise. Examples: TimeGrad (autoregressive), CSDI (imputation: fill in missing parts of a path), TSDiff (unconditional model steered at sampling time).

  • What it adds: joint paths for all four prices at once, spike clustering and tail dependence, no Gaussian assumption.
  • Conditioning on a prefix (CSDI-style imputation) gives \(\mathbb P(\lambda_{t+1:T} \mid \lambda_{1:t})\) directly: exactly the conditional samples that learnt transitions or LSMC need.
  • Limits: few years of data with a regime break in 2023, heavy compute per issue, and hard calibration. A cheaper intermediate is a vine or t-copula on top of Chronos-2 marginals (tail dependence, still fast).

F5Mixtures of experts and ensembles

FixesRegime-dependent accuracy
EffortSmall (ensemble) to large (MoE model)
SDP fitDirect: better marginals

Two different things share the name:

  • MoE inside a foundation model. Time-MoE and Moirai-MoE route each input to a few specialised expert sub-networks. It's a model choice: swap it in and compare downstream profit, like any other foundation model (TimesFM, Moirai, TiRex, Toto).
  • Our own mixture over forecasters. Experts: Chronos-2, quantile gradient boosting, EMC pre-dispatch (once archived), persistence. A gate weights them by context \(x_t\) (hour, regime probability, reserve margin), averaging quantiles:
    \[ \hat F^{-1}(u \mid x_t) = \sum_{k} w_k(x_t)\, \hat F_k^{-1}(u), \qquad \sum_k w_k = 1,\ w_k \ge 0, \]
    with weights learnt online on the CRPS (F1). Quantile averaging keeps the marginals well-shaped, and the gate lets the spike expert take over when the system is tight.

F6Covariates

  • EMC pre-dispatch (prices, demand, reserve requirements): the market's own forecast. The hackathon provides “pre-dispatch market information”, so this is the first covariate to add when the data arrives.
  • Demand forecasts (known future) instead of past demand only.
  • Outage plans, intertie status, fuel index: the drivers of tight periods and spikes.
  • Other weather providers (ICON, GFS, ARPEGE are archived daily by cron); Open-Meteo's free API is non-commercial, so check its terms for the hackathon.

§6 How to judge a forecast

  1. Profit first: rerun the year (forecast → SDP → offers → simulator) and compare against the current forecast on the same data. Differences of a few percent between runs can be noise; look for consistent gains across months.
  2. Marginals: CRPS, coverage of the 10–90% band, PIT histograms by lead time, spike hit rate.
  3. Dependence: lag-1 and partial autocorrelation of scenario paths vs realised; the energy score and the variogram score of paths, which penalise wrong dependence between steps that marginal scores can't see.
  4. Means: bias of the forecast mean (not the median) for every product, since revenue is linear in price.

§7 Roadmap

Ordered by expected value per effort, for the hackathon (submission 30 Nov 2026)

  1. Pre-dispatch covariate as soon as EMA's datasets arrive (13 Nov).
  2. Calibration: ACI on the quantile levels, and forecast means for reserves (F1).
  3. Tail treatment beyond the 95% quantile (F2), needed before trusting the continuous price state.
  4. Check the Markov assumption (partial autocorrelation), then a regime-switching or two-factor state if memory shows (F3).
  5. Ensemble with online weights once a second good expert exists (F5).
  6. Diffusion paths as research after the hackathon (F4).
