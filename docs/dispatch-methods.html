Part 1 · Energy only

Dispatch Under Price Trajectories

How to turn a set of joint price paths into dispatch decisions and offers for a 100 MW / 100 MWh battery, method by method. Every method answers the same question differently: what does the battery know when it decides?

§1 The problem

Prices

Time runs over intervals \(t = 1,\dots,T\) of length \(\Delta = 0.5\) h (\(T = 48\) for one day). Prices are a random vector \(\boldsymbol\lambda = (\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_T)\) in SGD/MWh. The forecast gives us its joint law as \(M\) trajectories with weights:

\[ \boldsymbol\lambda^{\omega} = (\lambda^{\omega}_1,\dots,\lambda^{\omega}_T), \qquad \Pr(\boldsymbol\lambda = \boldsymbol\lambda^{\omega}) = p_\omega, \qquad \omega = 1,\dots,M. \]

These are whole paths, so they carry the dependence between steps: a high price at 18:00 makes a high price at 18:30 more likely.

Battery

State: the stored energy \(s_t\) (MWh) at the start of interval \(t\). Actions: charge \(c_t\) and discharge \(d_t\) (MW). One binary \(u_t\) forbids doing both at once.

\[ \begin{aligned} s_{t+1} &= s_t + \Delta\Big(\eta_c\, c_t - \frac{d_t}{\eta_d}\Big), \qquad \underline S \le s_{t+1} \le \overline S,\\ 0 \le c_t &\le \overline P\, u_t, \qquad 0 \le d_t \le \overline P\,(1-u_t), \qquad u_t \in \{0,1\}. \end{aligned} \]

Write \(a_t = (c_t, d_t)\) and \(\mathcal A(s_t)\) for the actions that keep all of this true. The cash in interval \(t\) is the energy sold minus wear:

\[ r(\lambda_t, a_t) = \lambda_t\,(d_t - c_t)\,\Delta \;-\; \kappa\,(c_t + d_t)\,\Delta. \]

Energy left at the end is worth \(\Phi(s_{T+1})\): a terminal value, or a penalty below the starting SoC.

Information

This is the part that decides everything. Let \(\mathcal F_t\) be what is known when deciding \(a_t\): the prices up to and including \(t\), and every forecast and covariate published by then:

\[ \mathcal F_t = \sigma\big(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_t,\ \text{forecasts and covariates issued up to } t\big). \]

A policy \(\pi = (\pi_1,\dots,\pi_T)\) maps what is known to an action: \(a_t = \pi_t(s_t, \mathcal F_t)\). It is non-anticipative: it never uses the realised \(\lambda_{t+1},\dots,\lambda_T\). The problem is

\[ V^\star \;=\; \max_{\pi\ \text{non-anticipative}} \; \mathbb E\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T} r\big(\lambda_t, \pi_t(s_t,\mathcal F_t)\big) + \Phi(s_{T+1})\Big]. \]

Non-anticipative still uses the forecast

The forecast is part of \(\mathcal F_t\): it is the conditional law of future prices given what is known at \(t\). A non-anticipative policy looks ahead through \(\mathbb E[V_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t]\), computed with the forecast's trajectories. What it may not do is act differently depending on which trajectory will actually happen.

Example: at 17:00 the forecast gives a 50% chance of a 19:00 spike. A non-anticipative policy picks one SoC at 17:00 for both cases. An anticipative model stays full only in the spike trajectories and discharges early in the others, booking both profits. No real battery can earn that.

Why “knows \(\lambda_t\)” is right with offers

In NEMS the offer for interval \(t\) is fixed at gate closure, two intervals earlier, so the battery doesn't know \(\lambda_t\) when it submits. But it submits a curve \(q_t(\cdot)\), and the market picks the point \(q_t(\lambda_t)\) once the price is set. A price-taker's offer curve is exactly a policy that reacts to \(\lambda_t\) while using only the information \(\mathcal F_{t-2}\) available at gate closure:

\[ a_t = q_t(\lambda_t), \qquad q_t \text{ chosen with } \mathcal F_{t-2}. \]

So every method below yields offers in the same way: it gives the best action for each possible \(\lambda_t\), and that response is the curve.

§2 Two facts that frame everything

Fact 1: a fixed schedule only needs the mean

Open loop means every action is fixed now and never changed, whatever prices turn out to be. Closed loop means an action can react to prices once they're seen. In bidding terms, a self-scheduled quantity (“sell 50 MW at any price”) is open loop, and an offer curve \(\text{MW} = f_t(\text{price})\) is closed loop.

Example

You hold 1 MWh. Sell it at 19:00 tonight, or keep it and sell tomorrow at a known 180 SGD/MWh. The 19:00 price is 100 or 300, 50% each (mean 200).

  • Open loop. Choose now: selling is worth \(0.5\times100 + 0.5\times300 = 200\), keeping 180. Sell: 200. With prices of 0 or 400 (same mean) the decision and the value are exactly the same.
  • Closed loop. “Sell only if the price is above 180”: \(0.5\times300 + 0.5\times180 = 240\). With 0 or 400 it becomes \(0.5\times400 + 0.5\times180 = 290\). Now the spread matters, because you can react to it.

In general: take any schedule \(a = (a_1,\dots,a_T)\) fixed in advance (open loop). Cash is linear in the prices, so

\[ \mathbb E\Big[\sum_t r(\lambda_t, a_t)\Big] = \sum_t \Big(\mathbb E[\lambda_t]\,(d_t - c_t) - \kappa\,(c_t + d_t)\Big)\Delta = \sum_t r(\bar\lambda_t, a_t), \qquad \bar\lambda_t = \mathbb E[\lambda_t]. \]

The best fixed schedule is the deterministic MILP on the mean price path. The spread, the quantiles and the correlation between steps play no part in it. (It should be the mean, not the median: with right-skewed prices the median understates every interval.)

Consequence. All the extra value of trajectories comes from adapting: changing later actions after seeing later prices. A method that doesn't model how it will adapt can't get value from the distribution, however rich the distribution is.

Fact 2: three values, in a fixed order

\[ \underbrace{\max_a \textstyle\sum_t r(\bar\lambda_t,a_t)}_{V^{\text{EV}}\ \text{best fixed schedule}} \;\le\; \underbrace{V^\star}_{\text{best policy}} \;\le\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_\omega p_\omega \max_{a} \sum_t r(\lambda^\omega_t,a_t)}_{V^{\text{WS}}\ \text{perfect foresight, path by path}} \]

\(V^{\text{WS}}\) (“wait and see”) solves each trajectory as if it were known in advance. The gaps have names: \(V^\star - V^{\text{EV}}\) is the value of the stochastic solution (what adapting is worth), and \(V^{\text{WS}} - V^\star\) is the expected value of perfect information (what no honest method can capture).

For a sense of scale, here are the realised energy-only results for our 12-month backtest. These are realised outcomes, not the model values above, but they sit in the same order: self-schedule on a point forecast 5.99 M SGD, water-value SDP 9.42 M, perfect foresight 16.65 M.

§3 The real difficulty: fan, tree, lattice

\(M\) trajectories describe the joint law. They don't say which of them the battery can still tell apart at time \(t\). That second piece of information, the filtration, is what every multi-stage method needs, and the methods differ mainly in how they supply it.

Full non-anticipativity on discretised prices with \(K\) levels needs the whole history as the state: \(K^{T}\) nodes, \(11^{48} \approx 10^{50}\) for our grid. Every practical method gives up something to avoid that:

  • it ignores adaptation (M1);
  • it adapts only once (M2);
  • it branches only a few times (M3);
  • it summarises the history in a small state (M4, M5, M6);
  • it restricts the shape of the policy (M7).

M1Deterministic MPC on a point forecast

InformationRe-forecast and re-solve; one path per solve
Uses the trajectoriesOnly their mean
In our pipelineSelf-schedule baseline

At each decision time \(t\), take the point forecast \(\hat\lambda_{\tau\mid t}\) for \(\tau = t,\dots,t+H\), solve the deterministic MILP, apply the first action, and repeat at \(t+1\) with a fresh forecast:

\[ \max_{a_t,\dots,a_{t+H}} \sum_{\tau=t}^{t+H} r(\hat\lambda_{\tau\mid t}, a_\tau) + \Phi(s_{t+H+1}) \quad \text{s.t. battery constraints from } s_t. \]

By Fact 1, \(\hat\lambda_{\tau\mid t} = \mathbb E[\lambda_\tau \mid \mathcal F_t]\) makes this the best fixed plan given today's information. The re-solving does adapt in practice, but the plan itself doesn't know that it will adapt. It gives no value to keeping energy in reserve for a spike that might come. Price-taking offers follow: sell the planned MW at the floor, buy at the cap.

Strengths

  • Simple, fast (~5 s for a year as one MILP)
  • Exact physics, binaries included

Limits

  • No option value; plans for the average day, which never happens
  • Offer curve is a step at the planned MW

M2Two-stage stochastic program (scenario MPC)

InformationNon-anticipative for the first decision only
Uses the trajectoriesDirectly, as scenarios
In our pipelineStochastic MILP reserve planner

Share the decision you must take now across all trajectories, and let every later decision depend on its own trajectory:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{a_1,\; a^\omega_{2:T}} \;& \sum_{\omega=1}^{M} p_\omega \Big[ r(\lambda^\omega_1, a_1) + \sum_{t=2}^{T} r(\lambda^\omega_t, a^\omega_t) + \Phi(s^\omega_{T+1}) \Big]\\ \text{s.t. }\; & s^\omega_{t+1} = s^\omega_t + \Delta\big(\eta_c c^\omega_t - d^\omega_t/\eta_d\big), \quad s^\omega_2 = s_1 + \Delta(\eta_c c_1 - d_1/\eta_d) \quad \forall\omega. \end{aligned} \]

Rewrite the value of a first decision to see the bias:

\[ Q^{\text{2S}}(a_1) = \mathbb E\big[r(\lambda_1,a_1)\big] + \underbrace{\mathbb E_\omega\Big[\max_{a_{2:T}} \textstyle\sum_{t\ge 2} r(\lambda^\omega_t, a_t)\,\Big|\, s_2\Big]}_{\text{perfect foresight from } t=2} \;\ge\; \text{true value of } a_1. \]

From step 2 on, each scenario sees its own future. The model overrates flexibility, because it believes it will always catch tomorrow's spike. Re-solving every interval helps, since only the first action is ever used, but the first action is still chosen against a clairvoyant future.

What we saw. The 20-path stochastic MILP planned reserves alongside clairvoyant per-path energy. Energy looked very valuable, so it held too few reserves: 31.44 M against 34.22 M for the SDP.

Strengths

  • Uses the trajectories as they are; no state model
  • Exact physics; any linear market rule fits

Limits

  • Biased toward flexibility (anticipative recourse)
  • Size \(M\times T\) MILP per decision (~1 s for us)
  • No natural offer curve beyond the first step

M3Multi-stage stochastic program on a scenario tree

InformationExact, on the tree
Uses the trajectoriesBundled into a tree
Typical size\(b^{k}\) leaves for \(k\) branching stages

Build a tree from the trajectories: paths stay bundled in one node until they become distinguishable. Each node \(n\) has a stage \(t(n)\), a parent \(\mathrm{a}(n)\), a probability \(p_n\) and a price \(\lambda_n\). One decision per node:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max \;& \sum_{n} p_n\, r(\lambda_n, a_n) \;+\; \sum_{n\,\in\,\text{leaves}} p_n\, \Phi(s^{+}_n)\\ \text{s.t. }\; & s^{+}_n = s^{+}_{\mathrm a(n)} + \Delta\Big(\eta_c c_n - \frac{d_n}{\eta_d}\Big), \quad \underline S \le s^{+}_n \le \overline S, \quad a_n \in \mathcal A. \end{aligned} \]

Writing it per node is the non-anticipativity constraint: two scenarios through the same node must take the same action there.

Building the tree from trajectories

  1. Choose the branching stages, e.g. after 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 h. Branching early matters most.
  2. At each branching stage, cluster the trajectories in a node by their recent history (k-means or the forward selection of Heitsch & Römisch). Each cluster becomes a child node with probability equal to its share.
  3. Between branchings, use the cluster's mean path.

Decomposition methods: nested Benders, or progressive hedging (solve each scenario separately, then penalise disagreement at shared nodes until they agree). The children of the root give the offer curve as points: one action per child price.

Strengths

  • Exactly non-anticipative on the tree
  • Uses the joint paths, full memory
  • MILP: binaries and any market rule

Limits

  • Size grows as \(b^k\); over 48 steps you can only branch a few times
  • Late stages become clairvoyant again (one path per leaf)
  • Results depend on how the tree is built

M4Stochastic dynamic programming on a Markov state

InformationExact if prices are Markov in the state
Uses the trajectoriesTo fit transitions (or a model does)
In our pipelineWater-value SDP

Suppose the price depends on the past only through a small state \(X_t\) with \(K\) values: \(\lambda_t = \lambda_t(X_t)\) and

\[ \Pr(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1},\dots) = P_t(j \mid i). \]

The pair \((s_t, X_t)\) is then a sufficient state, and the optimal value satisfies Bellman's equation, solved backwards from \(V_{T+1}(s, \cdot) = \Phi(s)\):

\[ V_t(s, i) = \max_{a \in \mathcal A(s)} \Big\{ r\big(\lambda_t(i), a\big) + \sum_{j=1}^{K} P_t(j\mid i)\, V_{t+1}\big(s'(s,a),\, j\big) \Big\}, \qquad s'(s,a) = s + \Delta\Big(\eta_c c - \frac{d}{\eta_d}\Big). \]

With \(s\) on a grid of \(N\) levels, a stage costs about \(K \cdot N \cdot N\) evaluations, plus \(K^2 N\) for the expectation. For us that's \(48 \times 11 \times 101^2 \approx 5\)M operations: milliseconds.

The water value and the offer curve

Let \(\bar V_{t+1}(s \mid i) = \sum_j P_t(j\mid i) V_{t+1}(s,j)\) be the expected value of energy left over, and \(\mu_{t+1}(s\mid i) = \partial \bar V_{t+1}/\partial s\) its slope, the water value (SGD per MWh stored). Comparing one marginal MWh of each action:

\[ \text{discharge if } \lambda_t > \frac{\mu_{t+1}(s\mid i)}{\eta_d} + \kappa, \qquad \text{charge if } \lambda_t < \eta_c\, \mu_{t+1}(s\mid i) - \kappa, \qquad \text{idle in between.} \]

\(\bar V\) is concave in \(s\) (relaxing the binary), so \(\mu\) falls as \(s\) rises, and the thresholds over the SoC range a tranche spans give a rising offer curve. With Markov prices \(\mu\) depends on \(i\) and so on \(\lambda_t\) itself: the curve is the optimal response \(q_t(\lambda) = \arg\max_a \{ r(\lambda, a) + \bar V_{t+1}(s'(s,a) \mid i(\lambda)) \}\) on a price grid.

Where the state comes from

  1. No state (stagewise independent). \(P_t(j\mid i) = P_t(j)\). This throws away persistence and creates spreads that don't exist. Our test: the SDP expected 25 M SGD a year where perfect foresight makes 16 M.
  2. From a model (what we do). Equal-probability bins of each step's forecast quantiles. Transitions come from an AR(1) on normal scores, \(z_{t+1} = \rho z_t + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\varepsilon\), discretised with Tauchen's method. This is exact if the copula's correlation is \(\rho^{|t-t'|}\).
  3. From the trajectories (scenario lattice). At each \(t\), cluster the values \(\{\lambda^\omega_t\}_\omega\) into \(K\) nodes \(\xi_{t,1},\dots,\xi_{t,K}\) and assign each path to its node \(i_t(\omega)\). Then count:
    \[ P_t(j\mid i) = \frac{\sum_\omega p_\omega\, \mathbf 1[i_t(\omega)=i,\ i_{t+1}(\omega)=j]}{\sum_\omega p_\omega\, \mathbf 1[i_t(\omega)=i]}. \]
    No parametric model: the transitions are whatever the trajectories do. This needs enough paths per cell (\(M \gg K^2\)), e.g. a few thousand copula samples.
  4. More memory. \(X_t = (\text{bin}_t, \text{bin}_{t-1})\) (order 2), or a slow level factor plus a fast one. That's \(K^p\) states: \(121\) for order 2, \(1331\) for order 3. Transitions come from Gaussian conditioning on the copula. It's worth it only if the forecast errors keep correlation beyond lag 1 that a single step doesn't explain (their partial autocorrelation).

Strengths

  • Exactly non-anticipative given the state
  • Gives the water value and a real offer curve
  • Fast for one battery (1-D SoC)

Limits

  • Only as good as the Markov assumption
  • Grid grows with every extra state dimension

M5SDDP (stochastic dual dynamic programming)

InformationStagewise independent, or Markov (MC-SDDP)
Uses the trajectoriesThrough a lattice, as in M4
Built forMany storage states (hydro cascades)

Same Bellman equation as M4, but \(V_{t+1}(\cdot, j)\) is not tabulated on a grid. It is bounded from above by cuts (planes). A concave function is the minimum of its tangents:

\[ V_{t+1}(s, j) \;\le\; \min_{k} \big\{ \alpha_{t+1,j,k} + \beta_{t+1,j,k}\, s \big\}. \]

Each stage is then an LP (binary relaxed):

\[ \hat V_t(s, i) = \max_{a,\,\theta}\; r(\lambda_t(i), a) + \sum_j P_t(j\mid i)\,\theta_j \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \theta_j \le \alpha_{t+1,j,k} + \beta_{t+1,j,k}\, s'(s,a)\ \ \forall k. \]
  1. Forward pass: simulate price paths through the lattice and the current policy, and record the SoC visited.
  2. Backward pass: at those SoC values, solve the stage LPs. The dual of the SoC balance gives the slope \(\beta\), which is the water value. Add the cut.
  3. Stop when the upper bound (the first stage's value) meets the lower bound (the mean simulated profit).

Strengths

  • No SoC grid; scales to many batteries or reservoirs
  • Gives both bounds

Limits

  • Needs convexity (LP stages): binaries and SoC-dependent rules must be relaxed
  • Same Markov requirement on prices as M4
  • For one battery a 101-level grid is exact and cheaper

M6Least-squares Monte Carlo (regression DP)

InformationWhatever features you regress on
Uses the trajectoriesDirectly, no discretisation of price
OriginAmerican options, gas storage valuation

Keep the SoC grid and the Bellman recursion, but estimate the conditional expectation by regression across many simulated paths instead of a transition matrix. Choose features \(Z_t\) built from information known at \(t\), e.g. \(Z_t = (\lambda_t, \lambda_{t-1}, \text{forecast mean of the next 4 h})\), and basis functions \(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_L\) such as polynomials or splines.

Backward over \(t\), for each grid level \(s'\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_t(s') &= \arg\min_{\beta} \sum_{\omega=1}^{M} \Big( \hat V_{t+1}(s', \omega) - \beta^{\top}\phi(Z^\omega_t) \Big)^2, \qquad \hat C_t(s', z) = \beta_t(s')^{\top}\phi(z) \approx \mathbb E\big[V_{t+1}(s') \mid Z_t = z\big],\\ \hat V_t(s, \omega) &= \max_{a \in \mathcal A(s)} \Big\{ r(\lambda^\omega_t, a) + \hat C_t\big(s'(s,a),\, Z^\omega_t\big) \Big\}. \end{aligned} \]

The Longstaff–Schwartz variant regresses the cash actually earned along each path by following the decisions, instead of \(\hat V_{t+1}\). That has less upward bias. Then judge the policy honestly on a fresh set of paths. The offer curve is \(q_t(\lambda)\), obtained by maximising with \(z = (\lambda, \dots)\).

What it changes. The regression is a conditional expectation given \(Z_t\), so this is still DP with a Markov state. The state is \(Z_t\), continuous and chosen by you, and its transitions are learnt from the paths without binning. It is the general version of the scenario lattice.

Strengths

  • Works with any trajectory generator, no Markov model of prices
  • Several features cost no more than one

Limits

  • Regression noise; needs \(M\) in the thousands per issue
  • Wrong features give a wrong value
  • In-sample estimate is optimistic; out-of-sample run needed

M7Direct policy search

InformationNon-anticipative by construction
Uses the trajectoriesTo simulate and score a policy
IncludesThreshold rules, tuned margins, RL

Fix the shape of the policy and tune its parameters \(\theta\) by simulation. For example, discharge above a price threshold and charge below another, both depending on SoC and features:

\[ \pi_\theta(s, Z_t, \lambda_t) = \begin{cases} \text{discharge} & \lambda_t \ge g_\theta(s, Z_t)\\ \text{charge} & \lambda_t \le h_\theta(s, Z_t)\\ \text{idle} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \theta^\star = \arg\max_\theta \frac{1}{M}\sum_{\omega=1}^M \sum_{t=1}^{T} r\big(\lambda^\omega_t, \pi_\theta(s^\omega_t, Z^\omega_t, \lambda^\omega_t)\big),\]

with \(s^\omega_t\) simulated along each path. The policy only sees \(Z_t\) and \(\lambda_t\), so it can't cheat, and its score on fresh paths is an honest lower bound on \(V^\star\). Optimisers: CMA-ES, Bayesian optimisation, or gradients through a smooth simulator. Reinforcement learning is this with a neural network as the policy.

Strengths

  • No state model, no anticipation
  • Tests the real offer format and market rules in the simulator

Limits

  • Capped by the policy family
  • Slow, noisy optimisation; risk of overfitting the sample
  • Best for tuning a few knobs on top of M4, not as the engine

M8Information-relaxation duality (how far from optimal?)

RoleUpper bound, diagnostic only
Uses the trajectoriesPerfect foresight with a penalty
Pairs withAny value estimate from M4–M6

Perfect foresight \(V^{\text{WS}}\) is a loose upper bound. Tighten it by charging the clairvoyant solver for using future information. For any value approximation \(W_{t+1}\) (e.g. from M4), define the penalty

\[ z_t(a) = W_{t+1}\big(s_{t+1}, \lambda_{1:t+1}\big) - \mathbb E\big[W_{t+1}(s_{t+1}, \lambda_{1:t+1}) \,\big|\, \mathcal F_t\big]. \]

It averages to zero for any non-anticipative policy, so

\[ V^\star \;\le\; \mathbb E_\omega\Big[ \max_{a} \sum_t \big( r(\lambda^\omega_t, a_t) - z^\omega_t(a) \big) + \Phi(s_{T+1}) \Big]. \]

With \(z = 0\) this is perfect foresight. With a good \(W\) the bound comes close to \(V^\star\). The distance between this bound and a policy's simulated profit says how much any method could still gain.

M9Continuous price state (fitted value iteration)

InformationExact given the state; the state is continuous
Uses the trajectoriesThrough a pluggable expectation
RelationGeneralises M4; M6 is one of its expectation sources

M4 bins the price state. Here the state is a continuous score \(z\) (or a few of them). The SoC keeps its grid, because the value is concave and piecewise linear in \(s\) and 101 levels are already near exact. The value is a function of \(z\), stored at nodes \(z_1 < \dots < z_J\) and interpolated between them:

\[ V_t(s, z) \;\approx\; \sum_{l=1}^{J} V_t(s, z_l)\, B_l(z), \qquad B_l = \text{hat function of node } l \ \ (\textstyle\sum_l B_l \equiv 1,\ B_l \ge 0). \]

The price at a score comes from the forecast's own quantile function, \(\lambda_t(z) = F_t^{-1}(\Phi(z))\), so the marginal of every interval is exactly the forecast's. Bellman at the nodes:

\[ V_t(s, z_j) = \max_{a\in\mathcal A(s)} \Big\{ r\big(\lambda_t(z_j), a\big) + C_t\big(s'(s,a), z_j\big) \Big\}, \qquad C_t(s', z) = \mathbb E\big[V_{t+1}(s', Z_{t+1}) \,\big|\, Z_t = z\big]. \]

The expectation is the only part that needs a model

Interpolation makes \(C_t\) a weighted sum of node values, \(C_t(s', z_j) = \sum_l T_t[j, l]\, V_{t+1}(s', z_l)\), with \(T_t[j,l] = \mathbb E[B_l(Z_{t+1}) \mid Z_t = z_j]\). Whatever supplies \(T_t\), the solver is unchanged:

  1. From a model (closed form). Gaussian AR(1) on scores: \(Z_{t+1} \mid z \sim \mathcal N(\rho z, 1-\rho^2)\). Each hat is a second difference of ramps, so with \(G(x) = \mathbb E[(Z_{t+1}-x)^+]\), known in closed form, \(T_t[j,l] = \big(G(z_{l-1}) - 2G(z_l) + G(z_{l+1})\big)/h\). No sampling, and the rows are exact probabilities.
  2. From trajectories, nonparametric. Kernel regression on the paths: \(T_t[j,l] = \sum_\omega K_h(Z^\omega_t - z_j)\, B_l(Z^\omega_{t+1}) \,/\, \sum_\omega K_h(Z^\omega_t - z_j)\). Rows stay non-negative and sum to 1, and there's no AR(1).
  3. From trajectories, by least squares (M6): polynomial regression of \(V_{t+1}(s', Z^\omega_{t+1})\) on \(\phi(Z^\omega_t)\). It's more flexible, but the weights can go negative, so concavity in \(s\) is no longer guaranteed.

Why linear interpolation

With hat functions, \(T_t\) is a stochastic matrix (rows non-negative, summing to 1). So a continuous-state DP with linear interpolation runs on exactly the same code as a Markov lattice. The state prices become exact quantiles at the nodes instead of bin means, and the transitions are exact hat expectations instead of Tauchen bins. A mix of concave functions is concave, so V stays concave in SoC and the water value keeps falling.

Node prices: use the local mean \(\tilde\lambda_{t,l} = \mathbb E[\lambda_t(Z) B_l(Z)] / \mathbb E[B_l(Z)]\), so that \(\sum_l w_l \tilde\lambda_{t,l} = \mathbb E[\lambda_t]\) exactly (Fact 1). The linear spreading adds a little variance per step, about \(h^2/6\) for node spacing \(h\). Taking it out of the AR(1) noise keeps the score's distribution stable across stages.

Strengths

  • No binning error; more nodes cost little (\(J \cdot N^2\) per stage)
  • The dependence model is a plug-in, swapped for trajectories when they're good
  • Extends to 2–3 coordinates (energy score, reserve factor)

Limits

  • Grows as \(J^d\) with the state dimension (tensor nodes)
  • Trajectory-based expectations need thousands of paths

§5 Where our pipeline sits

We run M4 inside an M1-style loop. Every 30 minutes a new forecast arrives, an SDP is solved on an 11-state lattice built from that forecast (route 2 in M4), and only the next offer curve is used. The real new information comes from the next forecast. The lattice inside one issue only has to describe how uncertainty resolves over the coming hours, which is what sets the water value's shape and the value of waiting.

Natural next steps within this framework

  1. Check the Markov assumption: compare the partial autocorrelation of forecast errors at lags 2–3 with zero. If it is near zero, the order-1 lattice is the right model.
  2. Continuous price state (M9): exact node prices and hat transitions on the same solver, with the expectation as a plug-in (in progress).
  3. Scenario lattice from trajectories (M4, route 3): fit \(P_t(j\mid i)\) by counting on a few thousand copula paths instead of using Tauchen. Same SDP code, transitions from the joint model itself.
  4. Order-2 state if step 1 shows memory: 121 energy states, roughly 3 h per year with reserves.
  5. LSMC as a cross-check (M6): an independent estimate of the continuation value from raw paths.
  6. Duality bound (M8): tells us how much of the gap to perfect foresight is reachable at all.
Part 2 · Reserves and regulation

Adding Ancillary Services

Same battery, same information problem, three more products. This part covers only what changes: the problem, the facts, the information state, and each method.

§7 What changes, in one list

  1. The price becomes a vector. \(\boldsymbol\lambda_t = (\lambda^{E}_t, \lambda^{\text{reg}}_t, \lambda^{\text{pri}}_t, \lambda^{\text{con}}_t)\). Trajectories are now joint paths of four prices.
  2. The action becomes a vector. Energy \((c_t, d_t)\) plus reserves held \(x^{p}_t \ge 0\) MW for \(p \in \{\text{reg}, \text{pri}, \text{con}\}\).
  3. The state does not change. It is still the SoC \(s_t\), one number. Reserves don't move the SoC (except regulation energy), but they use up power and SoC headroom.
  4. New coupling constraints. Per-class power limits (RC383) and SoC checks from the start SoC (RC386) tie energy and reserves together.
  5. The information state must cover four prices. This is the real cost: a lattice over four prices grows as \(K^4\).
  6. Offers are separate curves per product. Up to 5 pairs each, and the engine co-optimises and cuts to the SoC. You can't submit a joint response to all four prices.
  7. Price-taking stops being plausible. 100 MW against a 108 MW regulation requirement.

§8 The extended problem

Cash

Reserves are paid for availability: price × MW scheduled. Regulation also moves real energy around the schedule (AGC). We assume shares \(\alpha^{\uparrow} = \alpha^{\downarrow} = 0.05\) of the regulation MW × interval, settled at USEP.

\[ r(\boldsymbol\lambda_t, a_t) = \underbrace{\lambda^{E}_t (d_t - c_t)\Delta - \kappa (c_t + d_t)\Delta}_{\text{energy, as in Part 1}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{p} \lambda^{p}_t\, x^{p}_t\, \Delta}_{\text{availability}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(\lambda^{E}_t(\alpha^{\uparrow} - \alpha^{\downarrow}) - \kappa(\alpha^{\uparrow} + \alpha^{\downarrow})\big)\, x^{\text{reg}}_t\, \Delta}_{\text{regulation energy}} \]

Activations of primary and contingency reserve are rare (0.2% of periods in 2025), and their energy is paid as ordinary energy. They are not modelled.

SoC

\[ s_{t+1} = s_t + \Delta\Big(\eta_c\,(c_t + \alpha^{\downarrow} x^{\text{reg}}_t) - \frac{d_t + \alpha^{\uparrow} x^{\text{reg}}_t}{\eta_d}\Big). \]

Power per reserve class (RC383)

Charging counts negative, so a charging battery can hold more raise reserve than \(\overline P\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d_t - c_t + x^{\text{reg}}_t + x^{\text{pri}}_t &\le \overline P, &\qquad d_t - c_t + x^{\text{reg}}_t + x^{\text{con}}_t &\le \overline P,\\ c_t - d_t + x^{\text{reg}}_t &\le \overline P. \end{aligned} \]

SoC checks from the start SoC (RC386)

Each check \(k\) says how many hours each product must be sustained (\(h_{pk}\), with \(h_{Ek}\) for the energy schedule). The energy drawn must fit above \(\underline S\), and the energy added below \(\overline S\):

\[ \frac{d_t h_{Ek} + \sum_p x^{p}_t h_{pk}}{\eta_d} - c_t\, h_{Ek}\, \eta_c \;\le\; s_t - \underline S \quad (k = \text{C1, C2, C3}), \qquad (c_t + x^{\text{reg}}_t)\,\tfrac12\,\eta_c - \frac{d_t}{2\eta_d} \;\le\; \overline S - s_t \quad (\text{C4}). \]

For a given start SoC everything is linear in the action, so the feasible actions \(\mathcal A(s)\) form a polytope. Every method keeps its Part 1 form with a bigger action and more constraints.

Worked consequence: regulation near a full battery

With no energy schedule, C4 reads \(x^{\text{reg}} \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \eta_c \le \overline S - s\), so \(x^{\text{reg}} \le 2.17\,(100 - s)\) MW for our battery (\(\eta_c = 0.922\)). At 90 MWh at most 21.7 MW of regulation fits, at 95 MWh 10.8 MW, and at 100 MWh none. A policy that sits near full gives up regulation, and the value function has to know that.

§9 The facts, revisited

Fact 1 holds per product, and conditionally

Availability pay is linear in each reserve price. For reserve MW fixed in advance, only \(\mathbb E[\lambda^p_t]\) matters. Given a price state \(r\), only \(\mathbb E[\lambda^p_t \mid r]\) matters:

\[ \mathbb E\Big[\sum_p \lambda^{p}_t x^{p}_t \,\Big|\, r\Big] = \sum_p \underbrace{\mathbb E[\lambda^{p}_t \mid r]}_{\pi^{p}_t(r)}\, x^{p}_t. \]

So the right point value is the mean, never the median. Reserve prices are right-skewed: the realised mean is about 14% above the forecast median for every product. Using the median undervalues every reserve MW. Our year: median 34.22 M, mean 34.49 M.

Reserves offered at 0 are open loop in their own price

We offer reserves at 0 SGD/MW (price-taking), with the MW fixed at gate closure. That decision can't react to \(\lambda^p_t\): it is open loop in the reserve price, even though the energy curve stays closed loop in \(\lambda^E_t\). A closed-loop reserve offer would be a rising curve \(x^p = g^p_t(\lambda^p)\), priced at its opportunity cost:

\[ \text{offer price}^{p} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{energy margin given up}}_{\text{power used by } x^p} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{value of SoC headroom held}}_{\mu_{t+1}\ \text{over the RC386 band}} \;+\; \text{expected activation cost}. \]

The market only takes one 1-D curve per product. The engine then co-optimises the split and cuts to the SoC. The joint response \(a_t(\lambda^E, \lambda^{\text{reg}}, \lambda^{\text{pri}}, \lambda^{\text{con}})\) can only be approximated by four separate curves.

§10 The information state in four dimensions

Independent \(K\)-state chains per price give \(K^4\) joint states: \(11^4 = 14{,}641\). That's 270 times our energy lattice, and too slow per issue. Reserve forecast errors are strongly related, so a common factor carries most of the information. In normal scores:

\[ z^{p}_t = \ell_p\, F_t + \sqrt{1 - \ell_p^2}\; e^{p}_t, \qquad F_{t+1} = \rho_F\, F_t + \sqrt{1-\rho_F^2}\;\varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad e^p_t, \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\ \text{independent}. \]

This implies \(\operatorname{corr}(z^p, z^q) = \ell_p \ell_q\). With loadings \(\ell = (0.63, 0.45, 0.78)\) for (reg, pri, con) it reproduces the observed error correlations: \(0.63 \times 0.45 = 0.28\), \(0.63 \times 0.78 = 0.49\), \(0.45 \times 0.78 = 0.35\). Then:

  1. Cut \(F\) into \(R = 5\) equal-probability bins. Transitions \(P^R(r' \mid r)\) come from the AR(1) with \(\rho_F = 0.75\).
  2. The state price is the conditional mean \(\pi^p_t(r) = \mathbb E[\lambda^p_t \mid F_t \in \text{bin } r]\), integrated over \(e^p\) by Gauss–Hermite quadrature. By Fact 1 this is exact for the revenue, not an approximation.
  3. The joint state is \((k, r)\): \(11 \times 5 = 55\) states, with \(P\big((k', r') \mid (k, r)\big) = P^E(k' \mid k)\, P^R(r' \mid r)\).

The product form assumes the energy and reserve chains are independent. The observed correlation between energy and reserve errors is 0.19–0.38, and we ignore it. A VAR(1) on \((z^E, F)\) would couple the two chains without adding states.

§11 Method by method: what changes

M1Deterministic MPC

  • The MILP gains the \(x^p_t\) variables and the RC383/RC386 rows. One MILP per decision is still fast.
  • It is still open loop in every price. By Fact 1 it should use mean reserve prices.
  • Firm reserves option: no credit from the opposite energy flow. Raise reserves must fit with \(d_t + x \le \overline P\), as if the battery weren't charging. This is safer, but holds less.

Ours: self-schedule 33.42 M (non-firm). As the reserve planner inside the water value: 32.99 M.

M2Two-stage stochastic program

The natural split: reserves are the first stage, shared by every path; energy is the recourse, chosen per path.

\[ \max_{x_{1:T}} \;\sum_\omega p_\omega \Big[ \sum_t \sum_p \lambda^{p,\omega}_t x^p_t \Delta \;+\; \max_{c^\omega, d^\omega} \sum_t \big(\lambda^{E,\omega}_t (d^\omega_t - c^\omega_t) - \kappa(c^\omega_t + d^\omega_t)\big)\Delta \Big] \quad \text{s.t. RC383/386 per path.} \]

Two biases, pulling in opposite directions:

  • Energy is clairvoyant per path, so it looks more valuable than it is. The plan gives too much capacity to energy and holds too few reserves.
  • Reserves for later hours are shared by all paths, so they can't adapt as prices unfold. They look less valuable than they are, adding to the first bias.

What we saw. The relaxed 20-path version held too few reserves: 31.44 M, against 34.22 M for the SDP.

M3Multi-stage tree

Unchanged in form: one \((c_n, d_n, x_n)\) per node, plus RC383/386 rows per node. The tree must now be built on four-price paths. Clustering in four dimensions needs more paths per node, and the tree grows quickly.

M4SDP on a Markov state

The stage problem becomes a choice of next SoC and a reserve mix. Split it in two. For a move \(s \to s'\), the energy flow is set, and the reserves that still fit form a polytope \(\mathcal X(s, s')\). Availability pay is linear in \(x\), so the best mix sits at a vertex:

\[ R_t(s, s' \mid r) \;=\; \max_{x \in \mathcal X(s,s')} \sum_p \pi^{p}_t(r)\, x^p\, \Delta \;=\; \max_{v\, \in\, \operatorname{vert}\mathcal X(s,s')} \pi_t(r)^{\top} x_v\, \Delta. \]

The vertices depend only on the grid and the rules, so they are computed once per grid. The reward for each state is then a small max over precomputed corners. Bellman over the joint state:

\[ V_t(s, k, r) = \max_{s'} \Big\{ \lambda^{E}_t(k)\, g(s, s') - w(s, s') + R_t(s, s' \mid r) + \sum_{k', r'} P^E(k'\mid k)\, P^R(r'\mid r)\, V_{t+1}(s', k', r') \Big\}, \]

with \(g\) the MWh sold on the move and \(w\) its wear. The cost per stage is \(K \cdot R \cdot N^2\). With numba that's 58 ms per issue for 55 states.

At gate closure, reserves go in at 0 before \(\lambda^p_t\) is known. So the mix is chosen in expectation over the reserve state, \(x^\star = \arg\max_x \mathbb E_r[\cdot]\), and the energy curve is the optimal response restricted to actions that still fit beside \(x^\star\). The backward pass lets \(x\) depend on \(r\), which is slightly optimistic compared with offering at 0.

Ours: point mean 34.49 M, 55-state reserve lattice 34.53 M

M5SDDP

Reserves fit naturally here. All of RC383/386 is linear in \((s, a)\), so each stage stays an LP with \(x\) as ordinary variables, and no vertex enumeration is needed. The cuts stay one-dimensional in \(s\). Two cautions:

  • The price state is still a lattice, so the four-dimensional problem of §10 is unchanged.
  • Relaxing the binary allows charging and discharging at once. Check that this doesn't create fake headroom in the rules.

M6Least-squares Monte Carlo

This gains the most from more products. A lattice grows as \(K^{\text{prices}}\), while a regression grows with the number of features. Regress the continuation on \(Z_t = (\lambda^E_t, \lambda^{\text{reg}}_t, \lambda^{\text{pri}}_t, \lambda^{\text{con}}_t, \dots)\) straight from the four-price paths. That needs no factor model and no independence assumption between energy and reserves. The stage max uses the same vertex trick, with \(\hat C_t(s', Z_t)\) in place of the lattice expectation.

M7Policy search

A good fit for the knobs the SDP leaves open: reserve caps by SoC band, how far below full to stay when regulation pays, and margins on gate-closure SoC. Our behaviour check found the water value at SoC ≥ 80 MWh 57% of the time (self-schedule 21%), where C4 squeezes regulation. A tuned rule on top of M4 could test that directly in the simulator.

M8Duality bound

Unchanged: perfect foresight in all four prices, with a penalty built from the value function. Perfect foresight with reserves is 42.66 M, so the bound tells how much of the 8 M gap is reachable.

§12 Price impact in reserve markets

For energy (about 7 GW of demand), a 100 MW battery is close to a price taker. Reserves are another matter:

Primary reserve~175 MW100 MW50.9 M SGD (reserves)
Contingency reserve~411 MW100 MW

Model it as a residual supply curve for each product. The price is set by the rest of the market given our MW:

\[ \lambda^{p}_t = \lambda^{p,0}_t - \beta^p_t\, x^p_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{pay} = \lambda^{p,0}_t x^p_t - \beta^p_t (x^p_t)^2 \quad \text{(concave)}. \]
  • MILP (M1–M3): a concave piecewise-linear objective needs no binaries. Easy.
  • SDP (M4): pay is no longer linear in \(x\), so the best mix can be inside the polytope and the vertex trick breaks. Fix it by splitting each product into slices, each with its own price, which brings back linearity with more corners.
  • The price state stays on \(\lambda^{p,0}\), the price without us. Historical prices are exactly that, so forecasts and chains don't change.
  • The offer curve matters again. With impact, a reserve curve chooses which price clears (supply function theory), not just whether to take it.
  • Crudest version: cap \(x^p\) at a share of the requirement. Estimating \(\beta^p_t\) properly needs the other participants' offers.

All the ancillary results here assume awards up to 100 MW per product at the forecast price. That overstates reserve revenue, probably by more than any difference between methods.
